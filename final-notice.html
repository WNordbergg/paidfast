How to write a final notice for payment (before it goes to collections)

The last friendly-ish email you send. Here's what it must contain — and what happens after.

What a final notice must include

  • The word "Final Notice" or "Formal demand for payment" in the subject line — ambiguity kills urgency.
  • Invoice number, amount, original due date, and how many days overdue it is.
  • A specific deadline ("within 7 days of this notice, by [date]") — never "immediately".
  • Concrete consequences you are actually willing to execute: interest charges, collections referral, pausing work.
  • An exit ramp: "if payment is already sent, reply with the reference" — this preserves the relationship if it was an oversight, and looks reasonable if the dispute ever reaches a third party.
  • Your full contact details and payment instructions repeated in full.

Template

Subject: FINAL NOTICE — Invoice #[number] ([amount]), [X] days overdue

Hi [client],

Despite earlier reminders, invoice #[number] for [amount], due on [date],
remains unpaid — now [X] days past the due date.

This is a formal demand for payment. Full payment must be received within
7 days of this notice (by [date]). If it is not:

  • late-payment interest will be charged from the original due date,
    plus recovery costs,
  • the matter will be referred to a collections agency,
  • and any ongoing work will be paused until the account is settled.

If payment has already been sent, reply with the transfer reference and
we'll stand down immediately.

[Your name]

What can you legally charge?

European Union: Directive 2011/7/EU gives businesses a right to interest on late commercial payments and at least €40 in recovery costs — no reminder needed for the interest to start accruing if your terms allow it.

Sweden: Dröjsmålsränta (delay interest) applies by law (räntelagen) from the due date, plus a standardised invoice fee (faktureringsavgift, currently capped around 180 SEK) — this is why Swedish slutpåminnelser routinely mention both.

United States: No federal default rate — check your contract first, then state usury/prompt-payment rules. If your contract specifies a late fee, you can enforce it; if not, interest may still accrue as damages in small claims court.

⚠️ This is general information, not legal advice. For large amounts, talk to a lawyer or your accounting advisor before escalating.

After the final notice: your realistic options

  • Collections agency — they take a cut (often 10–30%) but handle everything; good for clear-cut debts over ~$500.
  • Small claims court — cheap and surprisingly effective in most jurisdictions for amounts under the limit; you keep the relationship-damage since it's formalized.
  • Debt sale / factoring — instant cash at a steep discount; last resort.
  • Write it off — sometimes correct economically; log the lesson (deposit terms, milestone billing) so it doesn't repeat.
